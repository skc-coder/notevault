## 1. Evolution of Money

Money didn't appear fully formed — it evolved to solve a specific coordination failure in trade. Each stage below solves a problem the previous stage created.

### 1.1 Barter System

The barter system is a system of exchange where **goods are exchanged directly for goods**, with no intermediary medium.

Its core failure is the **double coincidence of wants** — for a trade to happen, each party must want exactly what the other is offering, at the same time. An ice-cream seller wanting wheat must find a farmer who specifically wants ice cream *right now*. This friction is what every later stage of money exists to remove.

### 1.2 Commodity Money

When a particular commodity is given the status of a medium of exchange, that commodity becomes money. Historical examples include cowrie shells (Kori), tobacco, cocoa, cattle, and animal hides/horns.

> [!note] Key Idea
> Commodity money works because the commodity itself has independent, widely-recognized value — you'd want it even if it stopped being "money."

### 1.3 Functions of Money

| Function Tier | Includes |
| :--- | :--- |
| Primary | Medium of exchange, Measure of value |
| Secondary | Store of value, Standard of deferred payment |
| Tertiary | Buying luxury/discretionary goods |

### 1.4 Metallic Currency

A particular piece of metal is given status as a medium of exchange. Early forms had no fixed dimension (punch-marked coins); later coinage standardized shape and weight.

**Full-bodied coins:** a coin's *intrinsic value* (value of the metal itself) is **greater than** its *face value* (value printed/stamped on it).

**Token coins:** the reverse — intrinsic value is **less than** face value. Modern Indian coins (₹1, ₹2, ₹5) fell into this category, and their size/weight was reduced by the RBI over time, with older, heavier coin series withdrawn from circulation.

$$
\text{Full-bodied coin: } V_{\text{intrinsic}} > V_{\text{face}} \qquad \text{Token coin: } V_{\text{intrinsic}} < V_{\text{face}}
$$

### 1.5 Fiat Currency

Currency issued by the government, given **legal tender** status to settle payments and transactions — its value rests entirely on government backing/law, not on any commodity behind it.

**Commemorative currency** is a special case: currency issued by the government to mark or celebrate a historical event or person, rather than for general circulation.

### 1.6 Legal Tender Classification

| Type | Definition | Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Limited Legal Tender | Can be used only up to a fixed number/value in a single transaction | 50p, ₹1 coins |
| Unlimited Legal Tender | Can be used in any quantity to settle any transaction | ₹500, ₹200, ₹100, ₹50, ₹20, ₹10 notes |

### 1.7 Fiduciary Currency

Currency issued by an organization (not the government) to settle payments, accepted purely because people **trust the issuer**. Examples: cheques, demand drafts.

> [!note] Key Idea
> A cheque is fiduciary money, *not* fiat money — it isn't legal tender; it works only because the payee trusts the issuing bank will honor it. This distinction is a favorite trap in MCQs (see DPP Q3, Q6 below).

### 1.8 Demonetization

Demonetization is when the government withdraws the legal tender status of a currency unit. Common reasons: curbing fake currency, fighting corruption, formalizing the economy.

India's demonetization years: **1946, 1978, 2016**.

### 1.9 Plastic Currency & Cryptocurrency

Plastic currency refers to debit/credit cards.

**Cryptocurrency / virtual currency** is a value generated by a computer program by solving an algorithm; when that generated value is used by people as a medium of exchange, it becomes a crypto currency. Examples: Bitcoin, Litecoin, Ethereum, Ripple, Dogecoin, Libra Coin.

- **Satoshi Nakamoto** — creator of Bitcoin.
- Runs on **blockchain technology** — a decentralised ledger system.
- **Mining**: solving a computational puzzle to validate transactions and earn a reward.

| Merits | Demerits |
| :--- | :--- |
| Anonymity — parties to a transaction stay hidden | No physical backing |
| Lower transaction cost | Can be used in terror financing, human trafficking |
| Lower transaction time | Price volatility |
| Seen as a candidate future currency | |

**Stablecoins**: a crypto currency whose price is pegged to a real-world commodity or currency (e.g., gold, USD, EUR) to reduce volatility.

> [!warning] Regulatory Note
> Private cryptocurrency is **not legal tender in India**. This is distinct from the RBI's own digital currency initiative below.

### 1.10 Central Bank Digital Currency (CBDC)

CBDC is a **fiat currency issued by the RBI** in digital form — the digital rupee (e₹). It:
- Is legal tender.
- Sits on the **liability side of the RBI's balance sheet** (just like physical currency in circulation).
- Uses blockchain-adjacent distributed ledger technology.
- Flows: RBI → Commercial banks → Retail/Wholesale users.

### 1.11 CBDC vs UPI

| Feature | CBDC (Digital Rupee) | UPI |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Nature | Digital legal tender, like physical cash | Rail for moving bank deposits |
| Issuer | Reserve Bank of India | NPCI (with banks as participants) |
| Settlement | Final at central bank level | Interbank via NPCI |
| Offline capability | Possible (tested in pilots) | Needs internet/mobile network |
| Anonymity | Can be designed for cash-like privacy | Fully linked to KYC'd bank accounts |
| Use cases | Offline payments, cross-border, programmable money, cash replacement | Everyday P2P and merchant payments |

> [!note] Key Idea
> UPI never creates new money — it just moves existing bank deposits faster. CBDC is a genuinely new *form* of central-bank money, parallel to cash.

## 2. Money Supply & Money Multiplier

### 2.1 Money Creation (Credit Multiplication)

When a deposit enters the banking system, only a fraction (the **Legal Reserve Ratio**) is held back; the rest is lent out, redeposited elsewhere, and re-lent — each round shrinking geometrically.

**Worked example from the notes** (Legal Reserve Ratio = 10%):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\text{X deposits} &= 1000 \text{ in SBI} \\
\text{SBI reserves } 10\% &= 100, \text{ lends } 900 \text{ to Aman} \\
\text{Aman deposits } 900 \text{ in HDFC}, \text{ HDFC reserves } 90, \text{ lends } 810 \text{ to Shri} \\
\text{Shri deposits } 810 \text{ in Yes Bank}, \text{ reserves } 81, \text{ lends } 729 \text{ to Ram} \\
\text{Ram deposits } 729 \text{ in PNB}
\end{aligned}
$$

Total new deposits generated so far: $1000 + 900 + 810 + 729 = 3439$, and the chain continues asymptotically.

### 2.2 Money Multiplier Formula

The money multiplier measures how much total money supply an initial deposit can generate in the economy.

$$
m = \dfrac{1}{\text{Legal Reserve Ratio}}
$$

With a 10% reserve ratio:

$$
m = \dfrac{1}{0.10} = 10
$$

So every ₹1 of fresh deposit can theoretically expand to ₹10 of money supply. In practice, credit creation is limited by:

- Banking habits of the population (how much cash people keep vs. deposit)
- The legal reserve ratio itself
- Availability of cash in the system
- Demand for loans
- Prevailing economic conditions

> [!note] Key Idea
> A *higher* reserve ratio shrinks the multiplier; a population with strong banking habits (depositing rather than hoarding cash) raises it. This is why DPP 02 Q7 answers "increase in banking habits" as the driver of a *higher* multiplier, not the reserve ratio.

### 2.3 Liquidity Ranking

Liquidity = how easily an asset converts into cash.

$$
\text{Cash} > \text{Bank Deposit} > \text{Gold} > \text{Land} \quad \text{(most} \to \text{least liquid)}
$$

Within deposits: **Demand Deposits** (savings/current accounts, withdrawable on demand) are more liquid than **Time Deposits** (fixed/recurring deposits, locked for a term).

### 2.4 Money Supply Aggregates (M0–M4)

Ranked top-to-bottom by liquidity (highest to lowest); amount increases down the pyramid while liquidity decreases.

| Aggregate | Composition | Classification |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| $M_0$ | Currency + coins with public + Bankers' deposits with RBI + Other deposits with RBI (= High-Powered/Base Money) | Most liquid |
| $M_1$ | Currency with public (CC) + Net Demand Deposits (DD) + Other deposits with RBI (OD) | Narrow Money |
| $M_2$ | $M_1$ + Savings deposits with Post Office | Narrow Money |
| $M_3$ | $M_1$ + Time deposits with the banking system | Broad Money |
| $M_4$ | $M_3$ + All Post Office deposits (excluding National Savings Certificates) | Broad Money, least liquid |

$$
M_1 = CC + DD + OD
$$

> [!note] Key Idea
> $M_3$ is the most widely tracked/used measure of money supply in India's monetary policy framework (this is what DPP 02 Q10 is testing).
