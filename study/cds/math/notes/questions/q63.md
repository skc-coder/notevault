# Question 63: Modular Inverse Pair Counting in Cartesian Product

## Question
Let $S$ be the set of first 14 natural numbers $S = \{1, 2, 3, \dots, 14\}$. The possible number of pairs $(a, b)$ where $a, b \in S$ and $a \neq b$ such that $a \cdot b \equiv 1 \pmod{15}$ is:
- (a) 3
- (b) 5
- (c) 6
- (d) None of these

---

## Detailed Solution

### Step 1: Condition Analysis
We are looking for pairs $(a, b)$ in $S \times S$ such that:
1. $a, b \in \{1, 2, 3, \dots, 14\}$
2. $a \neq b$
3. $a \cdot b \equiv 1 \pmod{15}$ (i.e. $a \cdot b - 1$ is a multiple of 15).

### Step 2: Testing Modular Inverses modulo 15
Notice that for $a$ to have a multiplicative inverse mod 15, $\gcd(a, 15)$ must be 1.
The numbers in $S$ coprime to 15 are:
$$\{1, 2, 4, 7, 8, 11, 13, 14\}$$

Let's find the inverse $b$ for each $a$:
- If $a = 1$: $1 \cdot 1 = 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{15} \implies b = 1$. But $a \neq b$, so $(1, 1)$ is invalid!
- If $a = 2$: $2 \cdot 8 = 16 \equiv 1 \pmod{15} \implies b = 8$. Pair: $(2, 8)$ and $(8, 2)$.
- If $a = 4$: $4 \cdot 4 = 16 \equiv 1 \pmod{15} \implies b = 4$. But $a \neq b$, invalid!
- If $a = 7$: $7 \cdot 13 = 91 = 6 \times 15 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{15} \implies b = 13$. Pair: $(7, 13)$ and $(13, 7)$.
- If $a = 11$: $11 \cdot 11 = 121 = 8 \times 15 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{15} \implies b = 11$. Invalid ($a = b$)!
- If $a = 14$: $14 \cdot 14 = 196 = 13 \times 15 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{15} \implies b = 14$. Invalid ($a = b$)!

### Step 3: Enumerating Valid Pairs $(a, b)$ with $a \neq b$
The valid distinct pairs are:
1. $(2, 8)$
2. $(8, 2)$
3. $(7, 13)$
4. $(13, 7)$

Total number of valid ordered pairs $= 4$.

Since 4 is not among options (a) 3, (b) 5, (c) 6, the correct option is **(d) None of these**.

---

## Key Takeaway & Pattern
- Always check the condition $a \neq b$ carefully! Elements that are self-inverses modulo $m$ ($a^2 \equiv 1 \pmod m$) must be excluded when $a \neq b$ is required.
- Self-inverses mod 15 are $1, 4, 11, 14$. Distinct inverse pairs are $(2, 8)$ and $(7, 13)$ giving $2 \times 2 = 4$ ordered pairs.
