# Euler's Totient Theorem

## Statement & Formulation

For any positive integer $m$ (prime or composite) and any integer $a$ such that $\operatorname{GCD}(a, m) = 1$:

$$\mathbf{a^{\phi(m)} \equiv 1 \pmod m}$$

where $\phi(m)$ is **Euler's Totient Function** (the count of integers $x \in \{1, 2, \dots, m\}$ such that $\operatorname{GCD}(x, m) = 1$).

### Euler's Totient Function Formula:
If prime factorization of $m$ is $m = p_1^{e_1} p_2^{e_2} \cdots p_k^{e_k}$, then:
$$\phi(m) = m \left(1 - \frac{1}{p_1}\right)\left(1 - \frac{1}{p_2}\right)\cdots\left(1 - \frac{1}{p_k}\right)$$

---

## Group Theory & Lagrange's Theorem Connection

Euler's Totient Theorem is **Lagrange's Theorem applied to composite moduli**:

> **Lagrange's Theorem Corollary:** For any finite group $G$ of order $|G| = n$ and element $g \in G$:
> $$\mathbf{g^n = e} \quad (\text{where } e \text{ is the identity element of } G)$$

### Derivation from Group Theory:
1. Consider the **Multiplicative Group of Integers Modulo $m$**:
   $$\mathbb{Z}_m^\times = \{ x \in \{1, 2, \dots, m-1\} \mid \operatorname{GCD}(x, m) = 1 \}$$
2. **Group Order**: The number of elements in $\mathbb{Z}_m^\times$ is by definition $\phi(m) \implies |G| = \phi(m)$.
3. **Identity Element**: $e = 1$.
4. **Group Closure & Inverses**: Multiplication modulo $m$ is closed and every element has a unique inverse because $\operatorname{GCD}(x, m) = 1$.
5. Apply Lagrange's Corollary $g^{|G|} = e$ to element $a \in \mathbb{Z}_m^\times$:
   $$\mathbf{a^{\phi(m)} \equiv 1 \pmod m} \quad \blacksquare$$

### The Modular Inverse Formula & Intuition

#### 1. Formula 1: Using Euler's Totient Theorem / FLT (Best for Exam Powers)

Since $a^{\phi(m)} \equiv 1 \pmod m$, split $a^{\phi(m)}$ as $a \cdot a^{\phi(m) - 1}$:

$$\mathbf{a^{-1} \equiv a^{\phi(m) - 1} \pmod m}$$

- **Case A: Prime Modulus $p$ (Fermat's Special Case)**:
  $$\mathbf{a^{-1} \equiv a^{p-2} \pmod p}$$
  *Example*: Find $5^{-1} \pmod{11}$.
  - $p = 11$ (Prime) $\implies 5^{-1} \equiv 5^{11-2} = 5^9 \pmod{11}$.
  - $5^2 = 25 \equiv 3 \pmod{11} \implies 5^4 \equiv 9 \equiv -2 \pmod{11} \implies 5^8 \equiv 4 \pmod{11}$.
  - $5^9 = 5^8 \times 5^1 \equiv 4 \times 5 = 20 \equiv \mathbf{9 \pmod{11}}$.
  *(Verification: $5 \times 9 = 45 = 44 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{11}$ ✅)*

- **Case B: Composite Modulus $m$**:
  $$\mathbf{a^{-1} \equiv a^{\phi(m) - 1} \pmod m}$$
  *Example*: Find $3^{-1} \pmod{10}$.
  - $\phi(10) = 4 \implies 3^{-1} \equiv 3^{4-1} = 3^3 = 27 \equiv \mathbf{7 \pmod{10}}$.
  *(Verification: $3 \times 7 = 21 = 20 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{10}$ ✅)*

---

#### 2. Formula 2: Extended Euclidean Algorithm (Bézout's Identity)

If $\operatorname{GCD}(a, m) = 1$, solve for integers $x$ and $y$ in:

$$\mathbf{a \cdot x + m \cdot y = 1} \implies \mathbf{a^{-1} \equiv x \pmod m}$$

*Example*: Find $7^{-1} \pmod{15}$.
1. Apply Euclidean division algorithm to 15 and 7:
   - $15 = 2 \times 7 + 1 \implies 1 = 15 - 2 \times 7$
2. Rearrange to look like $7x + 15y = 1$:
   - $7(-2) + 15(1) = 1$
3. Take modulo 15:
   - $7(-2) \equiv 1 \pmod{15} \implies 7^{-1} \equiv -2 \pmod{15}$.
4. Convert negative remainder to positive:
   - $-2 + 15 = \mathbf{13 \pmod{15}}$.
   *(Verification: $7 \times 13 = 91 = 6 \times 15 + 1 \equiv 1 \pmod{15}$ ✅)*

---

#### 💡 The Intuition: Why $\operatorname{GCD}(a, m) = 1$ Uniquely Guarantees an Inverse

Think of multiplying elements in the group $\mathbb{Z}_m^\times$ as shuffling a deck of cards.

1. **The Permutation Property:** When you multiply every element of $\mathbb{Z}_m^\times$ by $a$ (where $\operatorname{GCD}(a, m) = 1$), you are simply **reordering (permuting)** the set of remainders!
2. **Hit the Identity $1$:** Because it creates a complete permutation of all co-prime remainders, **exactly ONE element in the group will get mapped to $1$**.
3. **The Inverse:** The unique element $a^{-1}$ that gets multiplied by $a$ to yield $1$ is your **Modular Inverse**.

##### What breaks if $\operatorname{GCD}(a, m) \neq 1$?
If $a$ and $m$ share a common factor (say $a=2, m=6$), multiplying by $2$ compresses the outputs into multiples of $2$ ($\{0, 2, 4\}$). Remainder $1$ is never hit, so no inverse can exist!

## Concrete Example: $m = 10$ (Composite Modulus) and $a = 3$

Let's test this step-by-step with numbers.

### Step 1: Form the Group $\mathbb{Z}_{10}^\times$
Find all numbers less than 10 that are co-prime to 10 ($\operatorname{GCD}(x, 10) = 1$):
- Numbers: $\{1, 3, 7, 9\}$
- Group size: $|G| = \phi(10) = \mathbf{4}$.
- Identity element: $e = 1$.

### Step 2: Cyclic Subgroup generated by $a = 3$
Compute successive powers of $3 \pmod{10}$:
- $3^1 = 3 \equiv 3 \pmod{10}$
- $3^2 = 9 \equiv 9 \pmod{10}$
- $3^3 = 27 \equiv 7 \pmod{10}$
- $3^4 = 81 \equiv \mathbf{1 \pmod{10}}$ ✅

Subgroup $\langle 3 \rangle = \{3, 9, 7, 1\}$.  
Order of subgroup $|H| = 4$.  
By Lagrange's Theorem: $3^{\phi(10)} = 3^4 \equiv \mathbf{1 \pmod{10}}$.

## Grand Hierarchy: How FLT and Euler's Theorem Unify

$$\begin{array}{ccc}
 & \mathbf{\text{Lagrange's Theorem in Group Theory}} & \\
 & (g^{|G|} = e \text{ for any finite group } G) & \\
 & \swarrow \qquad \qquad \searrow & \\
\mathbf{\text{Euler's Totient Theorem}} & & \mathbf{\text{Fermat's Little Theorem (FLT)}} \\
G = \mathbb{Z}_m^\times \text{ (Composite } m \text{)} & & G = \mathbb{Z}_p^\times \text{ (Prime } p \text{)} \\
|G| = \phi(m) & & |G| = p - 1 \\
a^{\phi(m)} \equiv 1 \pmod m & & a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p
\end{array}$$
